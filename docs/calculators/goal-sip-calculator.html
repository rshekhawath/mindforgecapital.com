Why the goal is inflated first

A goal priced today will not cost that in ten years. An engineering degree at ₹20 lakh now is about ₹36 lakh at 6% inflation a decade out, and a plan built on the ₹20 lakh figure is short by nearly half. The calculator inflates the goal first and then solves for the instalment that reaches the inflated number.

target = goal × (1 + inflation)t  ·  P = target × i ÷ ([ (1 + i)n − 1 ] × (1 + i))

Reading the result

  • Monthly SIP required — the instalment that reaches the inflated target at the return you assumed.
  • Goal in future money — what the goal will actually cost on the day you need it.
  • You would invest — the instalment multiplied by the number of months. The gap between that and the target is the work the return is expected to do.

If the number is uncomfortable

There are only four levers, and the calculator prices each one: invest more per month, give the goal more time, assume a higher return, or want less. Moving the return assumption is the easiest thing to do on a screen and the hardest thing to do in a portfolio — treat a higher number there as a claim that needs evidence, not a fix.

Assumptions this makes

  • A constant return compounded monthly, instalments at the start of each month, and a constant inflation rate applied to the goal.
  • No existing savings are counted. If you already have part of the goal, work out what that grows to in the lumpsum calculator and reduce the goal by that amount.
  • No fees or taxes. Markets do not deliver the same return every year, so treat the output as an illustration of compounding — not a forecast, and not investment advice.

Questions

Should I enter the goal in today's money or future money?
Today's money. The calculator inflates it for you at the rate you set, and shows the future cost beside the answer so you can see what it did.
What if I already have some money saved for this goal?
Grow that amount to the goal date in the lumpsum calculator, subtract the result from the future cost, and enter what is left as the goal (in today's money, divided back out) — or simply reduce the goal until the required SIP matches what you can actually invest.
Why does a longer horizon reduce the SIP so sharply?
Because the instalments compound for longer and there are more of them. Both effects pull in the same direction, so the required SIP falls faster than the extra time would suggest.
Is a higher assumed return a free way to lower the SIP?
On the screen, yes. In practice a higher expected return comes with deeper drawdowns and a wider range of outcomes, and the plan has to survive those. Assume conservatively and treat any excess as a bonus.
