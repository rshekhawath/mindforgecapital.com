How a lumpsum is calculated

One amount left alone compounds on itself. There is no instalment schedule to model, so the arithmetic is a single line:

A = P × (1 + r)t

where P is the amount invested, r is the annual return and t is the number of years. This calculator compounds annually, which is the convention for a lumpsum figure; monthly compounding at the same nominal rate would give a slightly higher number.

Lumpsum or SIP?

They answer different questions. A lumpsum is what happens to money you already have; a SIP is what happens to money that arrives every month. If you hold a large amount and are nervous about entry timing, the honest comparison is between investing it now and staggering it — not between "lumpsum" and "SIP" as strategies.

What inflation does to it

The maturity value is in future rupees. The calculator also reports the same figure in today's money at the inflation rate you set, because a number twenty years out is easy to misread: at 6% inflation, ₹1 crore in 2046 buys what about ₹31 lakh buys now.

Assumptions this makes

  • A constant annual return, compounded once a year, with nothing added or withdrawn on the way.
  • No expense ratio, advisory fee, exit load or tax. Those are deducted from the figure above, and a percentage fee compounds against you — see the fee calculator.
  • Markets do not deliver the same return every year, so treat the output as an illustration of compounding — not a forecast, and not investment advice.

Questions

Does this use annual or monthly compounding?
Annual. Each year's closing value is the previous year's value multiplied by one plus the return. Monthly compounding at the same nominal rate produces a slightly larger figure.
What is the inflation-adjusted value?
The maturity amount divided by (1 + inflation) raised to the number of years — what the future amount would buy at today's prices. It is the figure to use when you are planning a real-world goal.
Can I use this for a fixed deposit?
For a rough figure, yes — set the return to the FD rate. A bank FD usually compounds quarterly and the interest is taxed at your slab rate every year, so the number here will be a little optimistic for an FD.
Why does a small change in the return move the answer so much?
Because it compounds. Over twenty years, 12% turns ₹10 lakh into about ₹96 lakh and 14% turns it into about ₹1.37 crore — a two-point difference in the assumption is about a 42% difference in the result.
