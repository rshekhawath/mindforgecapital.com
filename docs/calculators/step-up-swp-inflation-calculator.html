How the income is modelled

The first year pays the income you enter, every month. Every twelve months the withdrawal is raised by the inflation rate, so each year buys what the first year did. Each month the balance first grows by one twelfth of the annual return, then the withdrawal is taken out; if the balance cannot cover it, what is left is paid and the plan ends there.

withdrawal in year y = income × (1 + inflation)y − 1  ·  balance = balance × (1 + i) − withdrawal

Why a flat SWP flatters you

At the defaults, ₹50,000 a month from ₹1 crore at 9% looks comfortable as a flat withdrawal: after 25 years ₹3,80,28,048 would still be invested. Raise the same income with 6% inflation and the corpus runs out after 24 years and 9 months, because by year 25 the withdrawal is ₹2,02,447 a month. The flat figure is not wrong arithmetic — it is an income that buys about a quarter as much by the end.

Reading “today’s money”

A figure from year y is divided by (1 + inflation) for every year that has passed, so it is expressed in what it would buy today. The income itself always comes back to the amount you entered — that is the point of raising it with inflation — so the useful columns are the balance and what that balance would buy today.

What this model does not capture

  • Sequence risk. A fall in the early years, while an inflation-linked withdrawal keeps rising, does far more damage than the constant return here suggests.
  • A steady inflation rate. Prices do not rise by the same percentage every year. A few years of higher inflation early in the plan raise every withdrawal after them.
  • Tax. Each withdrawal from a mutual fund is a redemption, and the gain portion is taxable. The figures here are before tax.

Questions

How is this different from the step-up SWP calculator?
There you choose any step-up and read the results in future rupees. Here the step-up is the inflation rate, the income is entered in today's money, and the balance is also shown in today's money, which is how a retirement income is usually planned.
What does the most it can pay mean?
The highest monthly income, in today's money and rising with inflation every year, that the corpus can pay for the full period at the return you entered. It is solved so that the balance reaches exactly zero in the final month.
Which inflation rate should I use?
A long-run rate for your own spending rather than last month's figure. Costs such as school fees and healthcare have often risen faster than the overall index, so a rate a point or two above it is a common, cautious choice.
Is tax included?
No. Each withdrawal from a mutual fund is a redemption, and the gain in it is taxable, so the income you keep is lower than the amounts shown.
